with Content-Based Organization and Content-Verified Retrieval

Abstract
Institutional document stores are indexed by filename and folder
conventions, and retrieval stacks chunk them into flat passage
bags, so nobody can tell whether a retrieved passage answers
a query. We present Qdcvr, a deployable knowledge-base man-
agement platform that organizes documents by their content and
verifies every retrieval by reading it. Ingestion parses documents
into structured text, files each into a category base by reading
its content rather than its filename, splits long papers into parts
that keep their section headers, and propagates tags to every part.
Retrieval runs one query-driven protocol: vector-first recall, an
interpretable 0–8 content gate scoring the candidate text actually
read, a librarian fallback, and an explicit not-found contract when
no evidence passes. The platform is exposed to AI agents through
41 kb_* Model Context Protocol tools. On 50 real papers in 5 cat-
egory bases (165 indexed documents, 3.8 M characters, all parse-
verified), a scripted 10-question regression passes 9/10 and the
live run answers 10/10 on target (gates 6–8/8, two librarian res-
cues, top-1 gold recall 10/10, 1.6 s mean vector-recall time); three
out-of-corpus probes return not-found reports instead of fabri-
cated answers. Attendees can ingest, organize, query, and inspect
every trace live. Demo video: https://github.com/kingdol666/rag-
knowledge/blob/master/paper_demo/video/qdcvr-demo.mp4.

Introduction
Consider a research group holding 50 PDFs: an administrator must
assign each document a category and tags and, when someone
asks “what does X conclude?”, prove the right text was fetched.
Filename conventions and hand-built folder trees answer none of
this. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) promises answers over
such stores but inherits their disorganization.
RAG frameworks and integrated platforms chunk corpora into
flat bags of passages and feed top-scored candidates to the gen-
erator without a recorded pass/fail adjudication of whether the
text answers the query [9–12], a gap magnified by the documented
hallucination risk [18]. Structured variants add entity graphs or
summary trees [5, 6, 15], agentic and corrective methods self-assess
and re-retrieve [2, 7, 17], and selective QA formalizes abstention [8]
— yet the ranker still self-certifies by similarity. None packages ver-
ification as an auditable per-query artifact with a failure contract,
and none organizes the bases themselves by content.
We present Qdcvr, a deployable knowledge-base platform whose
organization and retrieval are driven by document content, not
filenames, fixed taxonomies, or bare vector similarity. Ingestion
parses each document into structured text [16], classifies it into a
category base by reading that text, and propagates tags to every
part. Retrieval runs through the Query-Driven Content-Verified
Retrieval (QDCVR) protocol, ending in cited evidence or an honest
not-found. The full stack runs on one host and is query-ready from
cold in ∼20 s. Figure 1 contrasts how a dense-RAG stack and the
Qdcvr protocol answer one and the same question; Figure 2 shows
the architecture.
• Content-based organization. A classifier reads the parsed
text and assigns each document to a category base (2 of 50
overrode the filename across categories, rationales persisted);
tags propagate to all parts (165 tagged).
• Content-verified retrieval. Vector-first recall feeds a 0–8
content gate (pass at ≥6), a librarian fallback, and an honest
not-found when nothing passes.
• Agent-native and deployable. 41 kb_* MCP tools [1] (94
in total) let AI agents operate the same platform as human
users.
• Evidence. On the 50-paper benchmark of Section 4, the
protocol answers 10/10 on target and surfaces its own misses,
probes, and per-question traces.
Section 3 walks through three live scenarios: content-based in-
gestion, gate-adjudicated answering, and a genuine not-found.
2
The Qdcvr Platform
Qdcvr is a single-host platform (Figure 2): a web console, a CLI,
an MCP server, a FastAPI backend, and storage — ChromaDB with
BGE-M3 embeddings [3, 4], a Neo4j graph [13], and a YAML-backed
file tree doubling as the audit log.
2.1
Lifecycle and Content-Based Organization
Documents enter through the web console, the CLI, or an MCP tool;
PDFs are parsed to structured Markdown with section headings
retained [16]; long documents are split at a configurable ceiling
(30k characters here) into (part k of N) fragments, each prefixed
with its section path, so a citation can name “part 2 of 3, §4.1” and
be read at an offset. All 50 benchmark papers parsed into 165 in-
dexed documents — 9 whole papers and 156 parts — covering 3.8 M
characters; a per-document probe verified all 50 after indexing.
Organization is decided by reading the document, not its file-
name: a classifier reads the parsed title and body and assigns each

Query-Driven, Content-Verified Retrieval
Phase 0 rewrites the query for retrieval. Phase 1 recalls vector can-

vector-first recall only. The content gate then reads the top candi-

not-found report naming what was searched and why it does not
answer.
2.3
Agent-Native Surface, Design Choices, and
Lessons
The kb-mcp server exposes 41 kb_* MCP tools [1] (94 in total)
covering CRUD, offset-addressed reads, tags, search, graph queries,
and an experience lifecycle that distils resolved Q&A logs into
reusable entries. A packaged skill encodes the §2.2 protocol so
agents reproduce it step by step instead of improvising a RAG loop;
the benchmark’s Track A is that skill. Deployment is one command
(ragctl); embeddings and parsing run locally; answer generation
uses any configured LLM (∼20 s cold start).
Every stage appends to one per-query trace — an artifact, not
a console log. Three operational failures shaped the design, and
each answer is a contract rather than a heuristic. (i) Chunk windows:
fixed-size windows scored abstracts and reference lists highest; the
part split, continuation reads, and librarian routing removed both
failure modes. (ii) Cross-base duplication: a sparse–dense hybrid
degraded once the same texts were chunked into parallel reproduc-
tion bases (7/10 whole-corpus, 3–4/10 per-base on our 10-question
check), so §2.2 contracts vector-first recall; repair under duplication
remains future work. (iii) Auditability: every gate score, fallback,
classification decision and not-found report is persisted, making
traces replayable rather than anecdotal. Three design choices fol-
low: whether a passage answers the question is a property of the
passage rather than of the embedding space, so reading replaces
scoring; bases are induced from content, not declared; and failure
is a first-class retrieval output, not generator prose.

3
Demonstration
Demo setup. The demo targets IR practitioners and research-data
administrators evaluating alternatives to folder trees, and agent
builders wanting auditable retrieval tools. The live instance holds
the 5 category bases with the 50 real papers (3.8 M characters)
plus three reproduction indices used for comparison (one under-
lies Track C). Attendees interact through the web console or any
MCP-capable agent harness driving the same 41 kb_* tools — one
protocol, two surfaces. Attendees may bring their own PDFs and
watch them parsed, classified, and tagged on the spot; all scenarios
run on one instance, no state reset.
Scenario 1: from upload to answerable. The attendee drops a PDF
into the console upload panel. Qdcvr parses it into structured Mark-
down, splits it into parts that each carry their section-path header,
and files it into the recommended category base, tags propagated
to every part — no dialog asks the attendee to pick a folder, schema,
or routing rule. The console lists the five content-classified bases
(72, 43, 32, 12, 6 indexed documents) beside the chunk-built repro-
duction indices. Point proven: the administrator writes no rules,
and re-filing is logged, not silent.
Scenario 2: gate-adjudicated answering. The attendee asks, “What
defines NISQ technology and what is its central limitation?” The
console shows Phase 1 recalling from all five bases, the NISQ paper
ranking first at 0.71 similarity, and the hit card naming the owning
base, document, and part (part 2 of 3). The gate reads that part,
finds the defining sentence plus the no-error-correction limitation,
and scores it 7/8 (an execution-LLM self-assessment, Section 4) — a
fast exit. Point proven: the answer carries a readable score and a
resolvable citation, not a bare similarity value.
Scenario 3: the gate discriminates, and failure is honest. (a) Rescue.
For the tumor-therapy question the initial top chunk is a header-
only part fragment; the gate scores it 5/8, the librarian fallback
re-searches and locates the abstract and equilibrium-analysis sec-
tions at 6/8 — named steps in the trace, not a silent retry. Two of
the ten questions take this path. (b) Honest not-found. The attendee
asks about a paper that does not exist: “What did ‘Spectral Tuning
for Low-Resource Odor Recognition’ report about training epochs?”
The trap is real — the top candidate is the BERT paper, which gen-
uinely contains training-epoch strings a similarity-ranked pipeline
would quote. The gate scores it 1/8 and the shelf scan confirms the
paper is absent, so the system returns a not-found report naming
its search scope, the near miss, and why it does not answer. Three
such probes ran (1/8, 1/8, 0/8); attendees are invited to pose their
own. Point proven: “I don’t know” is a protocol-level, per-query
recorded output.

Evaluation
Setup. Ten content-grounded questions go to three tracks over
the same ingested corpora: Track A, the Qdcvr protocol of §2.2
executed end to end by its packaged skill; Track B, a bare agent
with file tools over the 50 exported markdown papers (no index,
one fresh session per question); and Track C, dense retrieval over
800-character fixed chunks with the top-2 retrieved chunks packed
into a 4,000-character window for one LLM call. Every answer
is recorded verbatim as a repository artifact; Table 1 reports the
scorecards and Figure 3 shows the three tracks’ answers to one
question, side by side.
Results. Track A places the gold document first in 10/10 ques-
tions at 1.57 s mean vector recall plus 0.81 s of candidate reads, and
answers all ten on target. The scripted keyword regression passes
9/10; its single miss (BQ06) is a chunk-window artifact of the har-
ness. In the live five-section answers, all ten name at least one gold
keyword somewhere; seven name every gold keyword inside the
Answer section, nine once confidence and blind-spot declarations

5
Conclusion
Qdcvr organizes documents by content and verifies every retrieval
by reading: vector-first recall, a 0–8 content gate, a librarian fall-
back, an honest not-found contract — one auditable trace per query.
Figure 3’s tracks agree: the fastest failed twice (its abstentions be-
ing the very evidence-check the protocol makes explicit), the bare
agent answered without provenance, the protocol answered with
a citation — or a not-found report. The faster systems lacked a
component whose job is checking evidence — the barrier against
fluent fabrication.
