\begin{abstract}
Enterprise document systems must locate source material, establish whether it
supports a claim, report its absence. Retrieval-augmented
generation improves discovery but does not solve the problem: similarity
measures relatedness, not evidentiary support, and the stages that
supply it are usually built and evaluated in isolation. We present QDCVR, a
self-hosted knowledge-base system whose approach makes content the governing
object through three enforced contracts: content governs placement, content
governs admission, and absence is reported rather than replaced by plausible
text.

We evaluate the deployed system on three corpora differing in origin, language
and scale. At the tool layer every lane and lexical or dense baselines
reach similar recall; the informative differences sit elsewhere:
on identical candidate pools the same content verdicts hold 0.94 top-5 hit
rate when they admit evidence as a filter and collapse to 0.44 when they
re-rank it, so the verdict's placement, not its strength, decides whether a
citation survives. End to end, in a 30-cell comparison every arm
grounds all ten answers on the gold document, and the platform arm keeps
citation discipline (9 citations against 7 for file tools and 2 for dense
retrieval, whose markers point at chunks, not documents), at 52.6\,s and
\$0.71 per question against 22.2\,s and \$0.49. Across eighteen
adversarial arms no fabricated citation was observed; a reproduced
catalog truncation was repaired and passed a rerun.
These results support an operational claim, not universal retrieval
superiority: they show that trust depends on the whole path from indexed
content to evidence-aware answering.
\end{abstract}
