Abstract
Enterprise document systems must locate source material, establish whether it supports a claim, and report its absence. Retrieval-
augmented generation improves discovery, but topical similarity measures relatedness, not evidentiary support, while the stages that
supply it are usually built and evaluated in isolation. We present QDCVR, a self-hosted knowledge-base system that makes content
the governing object through three enforced contracts: content governs placement, content governs admission, and absence is
reported rather than replaced by a plausible answer. We evaluate the deployed system on three corpora differing in origin, language
and scale: retrieval-only behaviour on ten questions across three lanes and three baselines, three end-to-end arms on the same ten,
abstention on machine-verified in- and out-of-corpus questions, and lifecycle and pagination contracts. The retrieval matrix finds
similar recall across baselines and lanes, but answer-layer outcomes differ: in a 30-cell three-arm comparison every arm grounds
all ten answers on the gold document, and the platform arm alone keeps citation discipline (9 citations against 7 for file tools and
2 for dense retrieval, whose markers point at chunks, not documents), at 52.6 s and $0.71 per question versus 22.2 s and $0.49
on file tools. Across eighteen adversarial arms no fabricated citation was observed, though a partial answer exposed the limits
of binary found/refused grading; a reproduced catalog-truncation failure was repaired and passed a rerun. These results support
an operational claim, not universal retrieval superiority: trust depends on the whole path from indexed content to evidence-aware
answering.
