\subsection{Answer quality in the native deployment case study (E2)}
\label{sec:e10}
\label{sec:external-baselines}
\label{sec:exp-e2}
An agent can follow up a search by reading the source document, whereas a
one-shot baseline must answer from the passages in its prompt. We use this
comparison as a native deployment case study, not as a controlled test of the
full catalogue-first workflow. The configurations were compared on BQ01--BQ10,
drawn from a five-collection
arXiv corpus (100 papers, 328 stored parts and 9{,}504 passages). These
developer-authored questions were also used in development grids and
post-repair validation; they are not a held-out test set.

\subsubsection{Native configurations}
Configured for five collections, the deployed agent chose 2--7 MCP calls per
question (24-turn limit), using lexical-to-dense or vector search and document
reads. It made no catalogue or shelf calls: ``two-stage'' denotes lexical-to-
dense retrieval within one tool, not catalogue-first navigation. All three
gate calls failed, so E2 does not test the gate.

Its search scope was also wider than configured. The two search tools each
accept one collection identifier. In nine of the ten questions, 19 search
calls carried no effective restriction: 13 passed a list that the tools do
not accept and six passed no collection argument. The unrestricted calls
searched across collections. The scope audit reported 31 distinct
collection-ID values; individual IDs were not retained in the audit export.
Of 234 first-stage results, 139 had neither a
configured collection ID nor a recognised corpus identifier in their path;
of 180 vector results, 77 were identifiable copies of corpus papers in
other collections. These path-based counts are not semantic relevance
judgments. Document reads were narrower: 20 of 21 used the five configured
collections, and a path-based check identified all but one as gold reads.

BM25, dense search and RRF-order used the same generator and endpoint as
HMRS and indexed the intended 328-part corpus. Each received one prompt with up to twelve passages of 800
characters each. Okapi BM25 used $k_1=1.5$, $b=0.75$ and an ASCII
alphanumeric tokeniser without stemming or stopword removal \citep{bm25}.
Dense search merged the top 30 BGE-M3 results per collection by score
\citep{bge-m3}. RRF-order fused document ranks ($k=60$) and filled the
resulting context from BM25 passages \citep{rrf-cormack}.

RAPTOR, GraphRAG and LightRAG used the same capped prompt and BGE-M3
embeddings. The first two indexed a 30-part gold-paper subset; LightRAG
represented the full export in 699 chunks. DeepRead kept its own
locate-then-read pipeline in full-document mode. Its answer-time index
contained the gold papers, but parsing of the rest of its native corpus had
not finished (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). We could not identify a public
EPRAG implementation during the study period. The HMRS, BM25, dense and
RRF-order answers came from the earlier four-arm grid, and the external arms
were run separately; all 80 answers were scored together in the unified
blind pass.

Each question was scored in one blind pass. The judge, from another model
family, saw the question, a head-only gold excerpt and eight anonymised
answers, but not the retrieved contexts, system names or the passages behind
citation markers. At temperature 0, it was instructed to score each answer
on the four dimensions in Table~\ref{tab:rubric} (1--5, with 2--4
intermediate) without curving against the packet, and to justify every
score. Some saved rationales nevertheless refer to other answers in the
packet. The overall score is the unweighted mean. The judge also recorded a
holistic verdict (correct, partial or incorrect) and a rationale, separately
from the mean. An honest ``not in the material'' answer was not treated as
fabrication. Identifier presence, keyword coverage and gold-document reach
were recorded separately. These scores are not claim-level checks of source
support.

HMRS received ten correct verdicts and a mean of 5.00 in the unified pass
(Table~\ref{tab:external-baselines}). BM25 was close at 4.95. Against the
three baselines indexed on the intended corpus, the largest mean-score
differences were in completeness; none of those three paired contrasts
reached the unadjusted 0.05 level. None of the seven comparisons with HMRS
survived Holm adjustment. Earlier judge passes changed some dimension
scores and per-question comparisons.

Two questions show what the scores capture. On BQ01, BM25 received only
part~1 of the Transformer paper, whereas HMRS read further and answered both
parts of the question: finding a document differs from reading enough of it
to answer. On BQ07, BM25 cited material beyond the context it had been
supplied; we kept that answer as recorded. The HMRS agent issued two
searches in its first turn, read the first part of the
gold paper in the second and answered correctly. RRF-order and GraphRAG each
received twelve context units that did not include the gold paper; both
reported that they could not answer and were scored incorrect. The deployed
HMRS agent could read source documents and search outside the five configured
collections. Its estimated generator cost was higher than that of the three
baselines indexed on the intended corpus. The native comparison therefore
does not separate retrieval from differences in access.
