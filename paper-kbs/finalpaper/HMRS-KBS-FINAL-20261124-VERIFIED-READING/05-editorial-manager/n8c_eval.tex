\subsection{Using verifier scores to admit or rank evidence (E6)}
\label{sec:e6}
\label{sec:inflation}\label{sec:eval-verify}
A score used to admit passages need not work as a ranking score. We took
16 candidate pools from the historical hybrid wrapper and changed only
the order. Switching from fusion order to verifier-score order reduced
the number of pools with a top-five gold document from 15 to seven:
eight lost a hit and none gained one (exact McNemar $p=0.0078$;
Figure~\ref{fig:verifier}b).

The admission question remains open. All 191 scores in a separate archive
exceeded the 0.5 cutoff (Figure~\ref{fig:verifier}a). In a reconstructed
replay with fixed candidates, the filtered and unfiltered sets matched on
nine of ten questions, and answer grades fluctuated even for identical
packs. The reordering result does not show whether filtering helps.

\subsection{What answer traces and success status reveal (E7)}
\label{sec:e5}
\label{sec:agentic}\label{sec:abstention}\label{sec:composition}\label{sec:eval-agent}
\label{sec:sysarms}\label{sec:e7}
On a shared five-collection corpus, each of three interfaces included
citation markers in nine of ten answers. A marker records an attempt to
cite; it does not show that the source supports the claim. In three
industrial probes with no answer in the corpus, the instructed agent
reported not-found. Other absent-answer probes still retrieved candidates
above the threshold. These observations do not establish a server-side
abstention guarantee or make the retrieval threshold a test of answerability.

The deployment probes exposed a different ambiguity: global search could
report success after skipping unreadable collections. Scoped search with
forced reindexing recovered a missing hit, and pagination recovered a
shelf listing cut off by the output limit. Reporting the scope searched
and any skipped collections makes it possible to distinguish incomplete
search from a completed search that found no evidence.
