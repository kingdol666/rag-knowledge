\subsection{Answer quality in native configurations (E2)}
\label{sec:e10}
\label{sec:external-baselines}
An agent can follow up a search by reading the source document, whereas a
one-shot baseline must answer from the passages in its prompt. We compared
these deployed configurations on BQ01--BQ10, drawn from a five-collection
arXiv corpus (100 papers, 328 stored parts and 9{,}504 passages).

\subsubsection{Native configurations}
The deployed HMRS agent was pinned to the five collections. It made two to
seven MCP calls per question within a 24-turn limit, using two-stage or
vector search and document reads. It did not use the catalogue or shelf.
Its three gate calls all failed, so this comparison does not measure the
contribution of the gate.

BM25, dense search and RRF-order used the same generator, endpoint and
corpus as HMRS. Each received one prompt with up to twelve passages of
800 characters each. Okapi BM25 used $k_1=1.5$, $b=0.75$ and an ASCII
alphanumeric tokeniser without stemming or stopword removal \citep{bm25}.
Dense search merged the top 30 BGE-M3 results per collection by score
\citep{bge-m3}. RRF-order fused document ranks ($k=60$) and filled the
resulting context from BM25 passages \citep{rrf-cormack}.

RAPTOR, GraphRAG and LightRAG used the same capped prompt and BGE-M3
embeddings. The first two indexed a 30-part gold-paper subset; LightRAG
represented the full export in 699 chunks. DeepRead kept its own
locate-then-read pipeline in full-document mode. Its answer-time index
contained the gold papers, but parsing of the rest of its native corpus
had not finished (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). We could not identify a
public EPRAG implementation during the study period. The HMRS, BM25, dense
and RRF-order answers came from the earlier four-arm grid; the external
arms were run separately. All 80 answers were scored together in the
unified blind pass.

For each question, a judge from another model family saw a gold excerpt
and eight anonymised answers. At temperature 0, it scored factual
accuracy, completeness, groundedness and attribution from 1 to 5 and
assigned a correct, partial or incorrect verdict. It did not see the
retrieved contexts and could not resolve baseline citation markers to
sources. An honest report of missing evidence was not scored as fabrication.
Identifier presence, keyword coverage and gold-document reach were
recorded separately.

HMRS received ten correct verdicts and a mean of 5.00 in the unified
pass. BM25 was close at 4.95, and the clearest differences among the
matched-scope rows were in completeness (Table~\ref{tab:external-baselines};
Figure~\ref{fig:external-baselines}). HMRS tied or exceeded each arm on
these questions, but no matched-scope paired contrast was significant.
Earlier judging passes changed some dimension scores and per-question
comparisons; no contrast survived Holm adjustment across the full
exploratory family.

BQ01 illustrates the difference between finding a document and seeing
enough of it to answer: BM25 received only part~1 of the Transformer
paper, whereas HMRS read further and answered both parts of the question.
Figure~\ref{fig:hmrs-vs-baseline} shows how access differed on BQ07: HMRS
opened the gold paper before answering, whereas RRF-order and GraphRAG
reported that their supplied passages did not contain the answer.
On BQ07, BM25 cited material beyond the context it had been supplied;
we kept that answer as recorded. The deployed HMRS agent could read more
than the one-shot arms, and its estimated generator cost was higher than
that of the three matched-scope baselines. The native comparison does not
separate retrieval from this difference in document access.
