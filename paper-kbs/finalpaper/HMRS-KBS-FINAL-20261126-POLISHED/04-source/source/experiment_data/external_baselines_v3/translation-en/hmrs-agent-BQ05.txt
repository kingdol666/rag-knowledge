**Conclusion: using Maddison's (2010) European GDP data, Nielsen (2015) demonstrates that the three growth regimes on which Galor's Unified Growth Theory (UGT) relies never existed in Europe: growth in Europe was hyperbolic throughout -- there was neither any "Malthusian stagnation" nor any "take-off" transition brought about by the Industrial Revolution. The theory therefore conflicts directly with the evidence.**

**1. UGT's core claims (the object under test)**

Galor's basic assumption is three growth regimes: a Malthusian stagnation period (from 100,000 BC to 1750), a post-Malthusian period (1750-1870), and a sustained-growth period (from 1870 onwards); industrialization (1760-1840) was "the prime mover of economic growth" (Galor 2005a, p. 212), and the theory claims a "remarkable / stunning escape" from the Malthusian trap (Galor 2005a, pp. 177, 220). Nielsen points out that Europe, and Western Europe in particular, ought to have been the theory's strongest testing ground: if these mechanisms really exist, their effects should show most clearly in these regions (P0, the test design in the paper's Introduction).

**2. Method of refutation: the reciprocal-coordinate test**

The reciprocal of hyperbolic growth $S(t)=(a-kt)^{-1}$ is the straight line $1/S(t)=a-kt$. If growth really had a stagnation phase, the reciprocal curve should show randomly fluctuating "Malthusian oscillations"; if a take-off had occurred, the slope of the reciprocal curve should bend distinctly downward; and a straight line of constant slope, arbitrarily cut into two or three segments and declared to represent different growth mechanisms, is methodologically meaningless (Nielsen's own argument).

**3. Data results (key numbers, verbatim citations)**

- The 12 leading Western European countries (85% of the GDP of the 30 Western European countries): $a=1.147\times10^{-1}$, $k=5.961\times10^{-5}$; singularity in 1923; "the economic growth was diverted to a slower trajectory around 1900, bypassing the singularity by about 23 years". The fit over AD 1500-1900 was "remarkably good".
- The 30 Western European countries: $a=9.859\times10^{-2}$, $k=5.112\times10^{-5}$; singularity in 1929, diverted to a slower trajectory around 1900, about 29 years before the singularity.
- Eastern Europe: $a=7.749\times10^{-1}$, $k=4.048\times10^{-4}$; singularity in 1915, diverted around 1890, about 25 years before the singularity.

**4. The four resulting contradictions**

(i) No stagnation: over AD 1500-1900 growth rises along a stable hyperbolic trajectory -- "There is no sign of the existence of Malthusian stagnation"; moreover, "the alleged remarkable or stunning escape from Malthusian trap did not happen because there was no trap".

(ii) No transition point: "Absolutely nothing had happened at the end of the alleged Malthusian regime. There was no transition from stagnation to growth at any time." The Industrial Revolution "had absolutely no impact" on Europe's growth trajectory.

(iii) The opposite direction: after the alleged onset of the sustained-growth regime, growth in fact "started to be diverted to a slower trajectory" -- Western Europe from around 1880 onwards, Eastern Europe from around 1890 onwards.

(iv) The data handling itself is flawed: Galor repeatedly takes only four selected points from the Maddison data and joins them with straight lines, a treatment of the data that is "unscientific". The theory is thus refuted by the very data used in its own modelling.

**5. The author's conclusion**

"Unified Growth Theory does not explain the mechanism of the economic growth because its explanations are based on mythical features, which did not exist"; the theory "needs to be either thoroughly revised or most likely replaced by a theory supported by a professional analysis of economic growth data." This conclusion is consistent with the author's earlier series of studies (Africa, Asia, the former USSR, world totals, Western Europe: Nielsen 2014, 2015a-e).
