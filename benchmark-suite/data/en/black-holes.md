# Black Holes

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. The term was popularized by John Wheeler in 1967, although the theoretical roots go back to Karl Schwarzschild's 1916 solution of Einstein's field equations.

Black holes are classified by mass. Stellar-mass black holes, formed by collapsing stars, weigh from about 3 to 100 solar masses. Supermassive black holes, found at galactic centres, range from hundreds of thousands to billions of solar masses; Sagittarius A*, at the centre of the Milky Way, weighs about 4.3 million solar masses.

The first direct image of a black hole was released by the Event Horizon Telescope in April 2019, showing the shadow of the black hole in galaxy M87, which weighs about 6.5 billion solar masses. In 2022 the collaboration published a similar image of Sagittarius A*.

Hawking radiation, predicted by Stephen Hawking in 1974, implies black holes slowly evaporate; for a solar-mass black hole the evaporation time would be about 10 to the power 67 years, far longer than the age of the universe.
