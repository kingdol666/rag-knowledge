# Wind Energy Basics

Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity. The power available in the wind grows with the cube of wind speed, so doubling the average wind speed increases the energy yield about eightfold, which is why siting is the single most important decision in a wind project.

A utility-scale turbine today typically has a rotor diameter of 120 to 150 metres and a rated capacity of 3 to 6 megawatts onshore; the largest offshore machines, such as the Vestas V236-15.0, reach 15 megawatts with 236-metre rotors. The theoretical maximum efficiency of any wind turbine is 59.3 percent, a limit known as the Betz limit, derived by Albert Betz in 1919; modern turbines achieve roughly 45 to 50 percent.

Global installed wind capacity reached about 1 terawatt in 2023, with China, the United States, Germany and India the largest markets. Offshore wind delivers steadier winds and is growing fastest: the United Kingdom's Dogger Bank project, due to complete around 2026, is planned at 3.6 gigawatts and will be the world's largest offshore wind farm.

Turbines start producing at cut-in wind speeds near 3 metres per second and shut down for safety around 25 metres per second. Capacity factors average 35 to 45 percent onshore and above 50 percent for the best offshore sites.
