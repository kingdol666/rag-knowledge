# Orbital Debris Removal: The Kessler Cascade and Active Remediation

## The Kessler Syndrome

In 1978 Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais predicted that the density of
objects in low Earth orbit (LEO) would eventually become high enough that
collisions between objects generate debris, which in turn causes further
collisions. This positive-feedback process is now called the Kessler cascade.
The critical threshold is reached when the collision rate exceeds the natural
decay rate of orbital objects.

## The 2007 Fengyun-1C and 2009 Iridium-Cosmos Events

The single largest debris-generating event in history was the deliberate
anti-satellite destruction of the Chinese weather satellite Fengyun-1C on
11 January 2007, which produced an estimated 3,500 trackable fragments larger
than 10 cm. The second largest was the accidental collision between the
American Iridium 33 and the defunct Russian Cosmos 2251 satellites on
10 February 2009, which created roughly 2,000 additional trackable fragments.
Together these two events increased the trackable LEO population by about 70
percent relative to the 2006 baseline.

## Active Debris Removal Concepts

Because natural decay at 800 km altitude takes centuries, active debris
removal (ADR) is required to stabilize the environment. The dominant proposed
technique is the tentacle-style net capture, in which a chaser spacecraft
deploys a lightweight net to envelop a tumbling target, followed by a
de-orbit burn using a tethered propulsion module.

A competing approach is the ion-beam shepherd, first proposed by Claude
Bombardelli and Jesus Pelaez in 2011. In this concept the chaser does not
touch the target at all. Instead it flies in formation a few tens of metres
away and fires a focused beam of quasi-neutral plasma from a gridded ion
thruster onto the target's surface. The momentum transferred by the impinging
ions gradually lowers the target's perigee until it re-enters the atmosphere.

## The Energetic Cost Constraint

A central finding of the 2021 ESA feasibility study was that the specific
energy required for ion-beam shepherd removal scales inversely with the
square root of the target's area-to-mass ratio. For a typical spent rocket
upper stage with an area-to-mass ratio of 0.01 square metres per kilogram,
the required delta-v for a 300 km perigee lowering is approximately
180 metres per second, and the transfer takes between 90 and 180 days of
continuous beam firing.

## The 25-Year Rule and Its Limits

The Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC) 25-year rule
requires that satellites in LEO be de-orbited within 25 years of end of
mission. The rule is a mitigation measure only: it applies to future
launches and does nothing to remove the approximately 900,000 objects larger
than 1 cm already in orbit. Modelling by Liou and Johnson in 2009 showed that
even with full 90 percent post-mission disposal compliance, the LEO debris
population would continue to grow without active removal of at least five
large objects per year.
