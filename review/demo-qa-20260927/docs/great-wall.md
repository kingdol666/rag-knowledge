# Great Wall of China 万里长城

> Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China (retrieved 2026-09-27)
> 万里长城 / 萬里長城 — UNESCO World Heritage Site

The Great Wall of China is a series of fortifications that were built across the historical northern borders of ancient Chinese states and Imperial China as protection against various nomadic peoples from the Eurasian Steppe. Several walls were built from as early as the 7th century BC, with selective stretches later joined by Qin Shi Huang (220–206 BC), the first emperor of China. Little of the Qin wall remains. Later on, many successive dynasties built and maintained multiple stretches of border walls. The best-known sections of the wall were built by the Ming dynasty (1368–1644).

## Length and Extent

- A comprehensive archaeological survey, using advanced technologies, has concluded that the walls built by the Ming dynasty measure **8,850 km** (5,500 mi).
- Another archaeological survey found that the entire wall with all of its branches measures **21,196.18 km** (13,170.70 mi) — announced by China's State Administration of Cultural Heritage in **2012**.
- An extra 290 km (180 mi) of wall was discovered in 2015.
- Approximately **2,230 years** old for the oldest sections; the wall spans 15 provinces and regions.

## History

The Chinese were already familiar with the techniques of wall-building by the time of the Spring and Autumn period (770–476 BC). Qin Shi Huang conquered the opposing states and unified China in 221 BC, ordering the joining and extension of northern walls to defend against the Xiongnu. Transporting material was difficult, so builders relied on local resources: stone from mountain ranges, rammed earth (" hanshi ") for plains.

The Ming dynasty (1368–1644) substantially strengthened and extended the wall with bricks and stone — rather than rammed earth — because bricks were stronger and easier to work with. Sections near Beijing (Badaling, Mutianyu, Jinshanling, Simatai) date from the Ming era. An estimate from 2009 found that **22% of the Ming Great Wall had disappeared** due to erosion and human damage.

## Characteristics

- Watchtowers and troop barracks, garrison stations, signaling capabilities through smoke or fire (beacon towers) were built along the wall.
- **Sticky rice mortar**: during the Ming dynasty, builders mixed slaked lime with sticky rice (glutinous rice) soup to make an exceptionally strong, durable mortar — one of the earliest examples of a composite mortar containing an organic material.
- The wall's defensive capabilities were strengthened by natural obstacles such as hills, rivers and moats.

## Visibility from Space

The notion that the wall can be seen from the Moon with the naked eye is a myth: the wall is at most 9.1 m (30 ft) wide, comparable to highways and airport runways. It cannot be seen from the Moon. From low Earth orbit it is also barely visible to the unaided eye, though it — like many other man-made objects — can be photographed from orbit with a camera.

## Tourism

The most-visited section is **Badaling**, north of Beijing; it has received over **10 million visitors** in peak years and hosted state guests including presidents and royalty. Other restored sections include Mutianyu, Jinshanling and Simatai. UNESCO inscribed the Great Wall as a World Heritage Site in **1987**.

## Name

The collective name "万里长城" (Wanli Changcheng, "Ten-Thousand-Li Long Wall") appears in Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* (1st century BC), referring to Qin Shi Huang's wall. In English, both "the Great Wall" and "the Great Wall of China" are used.
